# Research & modelling

You don't need a research department — you need to stop designing for an
imaginary user. Even an hour of structure here prevents weeks of building the
wrong thing.

## Jobs to be done

Frame the product around the job, not the feature: *"When ___ (situation), I want
to ___ (motivation), so I can ___ (outcome)."* The outcome is the thing to design
toward; the feature is just one way to deliver it.

## Top tasks

List the handful of tasks that matter most — usually 3–5 cover the bulk of real
use. Rank them. The design optimises for these; everything else gets out of their
way. If a "top task" takes more steps than a rare one, that's a red flag.

## Personas — lightweight, evidence-based

Skip the demographic fan-fiction. Capture only what changes the design:
- **Goals** (what success means to them)
- **Context** (device, environment, interruptions, expertise)
- **Constraints** (accessibility needs, connectivity, time pressure)

One real behaviour beats a page of invented biography.

## Mental models

The design must match how the user already thinks about the domain — their
vocabulary, their categories, their expected cause-and-effect. Mismatches show up
as "confusing" UI that's actually a confusing *model*. Name things the way users
do, group things the way they expect.

## Competitive / convention scan

Users spend most of their time on *other* products. Honour the conventions they
already know (cart in the top-right, primary action bottom-right of a dialog,
etc.) and only break one when you have a clear, stated reason.

## Assumptions & risks

Write down what you're assuming and which assumptions are risky. The risky ones
are what to test first — with a prototype, a flow walkthrough, or five-user
hallway testing.

## Output of this stage

A short brief: the job, the ranked top tasks, the user's context and
constraints, the vocabulary to use, and the riskiest assumptions. Everything
downstream answers to this.
